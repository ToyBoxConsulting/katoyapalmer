A Reflection on Alignment, Accountability, and What Comes Next

A necessary recalibration.

Katoya Raquell Palmer · December 29, 2025 · 3 min read
Originally published on Substack, December 29, 2025.

I didn’t enter 2025 intending to burn anything down. I entered it trying to understand why so much effort, loyalty, and resilience kept leading me back to the same kinds of disappointment. What became clear—slowly, and then all at once—is that survival without alignment will cost you time, clarity, and sanity. This reflection is me holding my past self-accountable for what I tolerated too long, and setting standards my future self will not negotiate.

For much of this year, I chose quiet. I lived small on purpose—at home, in my own cocoon, writing, thinking, and protecting my peace. I told myself I had enough stories to tell, and that it was time to finally sit still long enough to tell them. Some of that retreat was necessary. Some of it was earned. It gave me space to recover, to be present with my son, and to make my life comfortable again in ways that mattered.

But I also recognize a familiar pattern: when trust erodes, I pull back from the world around me entirely. Comfort becomes containment. Safety becomes distance. And while there was nothing wrong with choosing rest, there were moments this year when I mistook isolation for alignment, and restraint for readiness. That is a pattern I am naming clearly now—so it does not quietly define what comes next.

One of the clearest lessons of this year is how much of my participation has been labor-first. I have shown up, built, contributed, advised, organized, and extended myself—often in spaces I was told mattered—without experiencing the kind of celebration that makes momentum sustainable. Not praise. Not applause. Presence. Invitation. Ease.

I am not unwilling to work. I have never been. But I am no longer willing to confuse constant contribution with belonging, or to mistake being needed for being valued. That imbalance—where effort precedes care—has shaped my professional pace, my friendships, and my willingness to move forward more than I realized at the time. It is difficult to feel in motion when every step requires proving you deserve to be there.

In 2026, being visible and in motion will not mean doing more. It will mean being more deliberately placed. I want to spend time in rooms where people look forward to seeing me—not because I am useful, but because I am welcome. That may look like deeper engagement in spaces I already serve, moving through my community with more openness, or allowing myself to be present where there are shared interest, shared energy, and shared regard.

I know how to move alone. I always have. What I am no longer interested in is a life where I am always the one initiating momentum without receiving it back. Discernment, for me, now includes asking whether a space has the capacity to celebrate—not just consume—what I bring.

This reflection is not an explanation of what happened, and it is not an invitation to speculate about my past. It is a documented transition. I am releasing the need to be understood by people who benefited from my silence, my patience, or my labor. I am also releasing the belief that endurance alone earns access to the rooms that matter.

Going forward, I am choosing environments that can meet me with reciprocity, relationships that do not require labor to feel safe, and a pace that allows me to be visible without being depleted. That is not withdrawal. That is alignment.

And it is no longer negotiable.
