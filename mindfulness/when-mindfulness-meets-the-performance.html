When Mindfulness Meets the Performance of Success

A reflection on identity, composure, and the cost of keeping it together.

Katoya Raquell Palmer · November 12, 2025 · 6 min read
Originally published on Substack, November 12, 2025.

Authenticity has a way of testing us—especially in a world that keeps asking us to perform.
Perform confidence.
Perform composure.
Perform success.

And for many of us, that performance becomes a kind of second skin, one we forget we’re wearing until mindfulness invites us to pause and ask: Who am I when I stop performing and simply allow myself to be?

I’ve spent years learning how to navigate that question across every part of my life. In some rooms, I’ve learned that being adaptable and being authentic don’t always look the same. I’ve walked into spaces where my spirit started to shrink before I even spoke. A quiet signal that I was about to perform.

Sociologists call that impression management, the instinct to adjust our tone, posture, or presence to keep other people comfortable. But what starts as strategy often becomes survival.

In mainstream spaces, I learned to soften to round my edges, adjust my tone, and prove I could belong without making anyone uncomfortable. And still, I was called too strong or not a culture fit.

In spaces reflecting my own skin tone, I finally felt free to be myself—bold, assertive, unfiltered. But even then, when I spoke plainly or shared an idea without code-switching, I sometimes heard, “You sound white,” or “You think you’re better.”

So, either way, I was damned if I did, damned if I didn’t—too much here, not enough there.

That’s when I realized: authenticity isn’t a luxury.
It’s a necessity.
It’s the only way to stop performing for rooms that will always shift their rules.

And like any discipline, medicine, law, and art; authenticity is a practice.
It’s a daily choice.
A decision to keep showing up as who you are, even when the world gives you every reason not to.

It takes mindfulness to do that. To notice the moment you start editing yourself and choose instead to stay whole.

That pattern has shown up in love, too. For a long time, I thought being easy to love meant being easy to handle. I played the part well—agreeable, quiet, soft—but all it did was teach people to love the version of me that asked for less.

When I pretended to need saving, I attracted people who needed control. When I showed up confident and clear, I was told I was too much, or too independent. Either way, performance stood between me and honesty.

Psychologists call that the fawn response the body’s way of keeping peace by shrinking to stay safe. It’s not manipulation; it’s trauma’s muscle memory. But mindfulness helps interrupt it.

Before any date or conversation now, I check in with myself:
Am I showing up real, or am I rehearsing?

Because love that demands a performance will never have room for my wholeness.
Real love doesn’t flinch at your brilliance, it recognizes it.

That same lesson echoes in my work and growth. Sometimes progress feels like fear. The first time I sat at a politically powerful table, I remember hiding my trembling hands under the desk. I thought that meant I didn’t belong.

But mindfulness reframed it for me. That wasn’t fraudulence it was expansion stress. My body was just adjusting to a higher altitude. Growth doesn’t mean you’re faking it; it means you’re evolving.

It reminded me of something Carl Rogers wrote about in humanistic psychology—how mental health depends on congruence, when who you are inside finally matches how you show up. That’s what peace feels like.

So now, when that old voice whispers, “Who do you think you are?” I breathe and answer, “Exactly who I think I am.”
That’s what self-trust sounds like.

But authenticity gets tested most when life gets heavy—when you’re quietly holding things together and people assume that because you look fine, you must be fine.

I’ve gone through hard seasons where I was struggling privately, still showing up for life, still smiling, still posting the highlights. And when support finally came, so did the comment: “You didn’t look like you needed help.”

That one stuck with me. It made me realize how often composure gets mistaken for comfort, and how the world sometimes demands we perform our pain to make it believable.

If you post joy, people say you’re bragging.
If you post struggle, they say you’re messy.
If you post nothing, they assume you’re okay.

That’s what performativeness looks like in the age of survival—the constant negotiation between what to share and what to keep sacred.

Mindfulness meets me right there, in the middle of that choice.
It reminds me I can be grateful and exhausted, strong and still in need, private and still authentic.
Authenticity doesn’t mean exposure—it means integrity.

The same theme has followed me professionally, too. There’s so much pressure to look successful and to post the wins but never the worries, to stay “marketable” even when you’re just managing.

But there’s a difference between being performative and being prepared. Performative energy seeks validation. Prepared energy seeks alignment.

I don’t owe the internet my breakdown to prove I’m real—but I also don’t want to curate my story so tightly that I erase my growth.

Mindfulness helps me find the line. Before I share anything now, I ask myself:
Is this honest? Will it serve others, or just soothe my ego? Can I share this truth without betraying my peace?

If the answer is yes, I share from my center, not my crisis.
Because alignment will always outlast performance.

Across all these places—community, love, work, survival—the same tension repeats: Be softer. Be stronger. Be more. Be less.

But mindfulness asks harder questions.
Am I performing to protect myself—or to please someone else? Am I telling the truth—or curating it for approval?

To live mindfully is to release the need to act our way through life—not by withdrawing, but by showing up whole. Because when we stop performing, we stop fragmenting.
And when we stop fragmenting, we start healing.

That’s authenticity.
That’s alignment.
That’s the middle I’m learning to live in.

Appendix: Theories & Frameworks Referenced

  1. Impression Management (Erving Goffman) – explains how people “perform” versions of themselves to shape how others see them.

  2. Double Consciousness (W. E. B. Du Bois) – the internal split of seeing oneself through one’s own eyes and through the eyes of others.

  3. Fawn Response (Pete Walker) – a trauma response involving people-pleasing or self-erasure to maintain safety.

  4. Authenticity & Congruence (Carl Rogers) – psychological well-being comes when our internal and external selves match.

  5. Code-Switching Fatigue – emotional exhaustion from constantly altering behavior to navigate racial or cultural expectations.

  6. Imposter Phenomenon (Clance & Imes) – self-doubt among high achievers who attribute success to luck or deception.

  7. Respectability Politics & Cultural Trauma – performing perfection as protection from systemic bias or judgment.

  8. Mindfulness as Corrective Practice (Neuroscience & CBT) – mindfulness retrains the brain’s stress response, turning automatic reactions into conscious choice.
