How Mindfulness Helped Me Shift From Hyper-Independence to Connection

Asking for Help

Katoya Raquell Palmer · September 23, 2025 · 3 min read
Originally published on Substack, September 23, 2025. Read the original

For most of my life, I’ve been the one people come to—for advice, for ideas, for help. That role has shaped my identity, but it also made it harder for me to admit when I needed support. Add the trauma response of hyper-independence, and asking for help felt almost impossible.

Part of why it’s been so hard is the way I’ve been met in the past. I’ve been told I needed mentorship, but rarely offered it. I’ve been dismissed for not having the right letters behind my name. I’ve carried the belief that asking would make me look weak. All of that kept me silent far longer than I should have been. And I’ve been sitting with this since August 22nd—my last full mindfulness post that wasn’t a teaser. It’s taken weeks of trial, reflection, and practice to even begin shaping this into something I could share. That in itself has been part of the lesson.

What I practiced this week was simple: pause, name out loud what I need—even if only to myself—and ask one person directly. My guardrail was this: if the first no shuts me down, then I remind myself the right yes might still be waiting. It sounds small, but saying the words out loud is often the biggest hurdle.

The science backs this up. Research on shame resilience shows that naming our needs reduces shame’s power. Vulnerability isn’t weakness—it’s courage. And my own legacy has reminded me of that. My son has been my teacher here. He is autistic and ADHD, and I moved him into a private school during the pandemic so he could finish middle and high school with the right support. He has always been so good at sharing his needs without hesitation. He knows his accommodations, asks for them directly, and uses his resources—so he tends to reach his goals. The only time it doesn’t work is when the community around him isn’t adaptive or appreciative. Watching him has forced us both to evaluate the spaces where we seek safety, work, and hobbies. And it’s shown me a truth I resisted: asking for help doesn’t make you weaker—it makes you more likely to succeed.

Here’s what I noticed when I finally practiced it myself. In my personal life, I felt more feminine energy show up. I wasn’t carrying everything alone. Spiritually, I felt more open. Conversations about energy and spiritual gifts flowed more naturally when I admitted I didn’t have it all together. Professionally, I was surprised by how much encouragement I received when I shared my business plan. People didn’t judge me—they fueled me. Before, I held everything in until I was desperate. This week, I asked earlier. And instead of spiraling, I was met with more support than I imagined.

Ultimately, asking for help showed me who values me—not for what I give, but for who I am. And that shift matters.

As I’ve been reflecting on how my son models mindfulness and how our communities shape what we learn about care and belonging, I’ve also started to notice something in myself. The way we show up—in family, in work, in public—can sometimes drift into performance instead of authenticity. Legacy and belonging have shown me a lot this week. But they’ve also raised a harder question: how often am I being my truest self, and how often am I just performing? That’s the reflection I’ll step into next.
